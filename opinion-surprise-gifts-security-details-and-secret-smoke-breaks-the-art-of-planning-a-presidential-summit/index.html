Opinion | Surprise Gifts, Security Details and Secret Smoke Breaks: The Art of Planning a Presidential Summit

Will Meet |   15 June 2021
 |  2011 words

The flags are raised, the tables are set, the policy notes are drafted. The Villa La Grange in Geneva, Switzerland, now awaits Presidents Joe Biden and Vladimir Putin. While all eyes will be on the leaders, the details that go unseen will have a significant impact on this highly awaited summit. From the seating to the greetings to the centerpieces, these elements of protocol—the rules that govern diplomatic interactions—have been in the works for months. Very little happens by accident in diplomacy. Everything is precisely choreographed, and every decision by the protocol team of each country has been carefully made to further specific policy goals.

“Protocol, shmotocol!” I heard this a million times when I entered my post as chief of protocol for the Obama administration. But many of these skeptics soon witnessed how the tools of protocol—location selection, arrival rituals, gift exchanges and more—allow leaders to connect from a starting point of mutual respect and broach difficult conversations without alienating each other. Protocol—whose ancient Greek etymology is a reference to the first sheet of papyrus that came at the beginning of an official missive—offers a shared blueprint everyone can follow, minimizing the possibility of disruptive surprises or missteps. When the building of diplomacy becomes wobbly, protocol is often the scaffolding that holds things together.

Diplomatic protocol obviously cannot eliminate the deep disagreements separating the United States and Russia. But the tools of protocol can be used to connect, to communicate clearly and precisely, and to move parties closer to mutually beneficial positions. At its heart, diplomacy is the art of building or rebuilding relationships. Protocol establishes the framework within which that hard work takes place.

As President Barack Obama’s chief of protocol, I had the great honor of welcoming high-level visitors on the president’s behalf, building bridges with foreign diplomats, and traveling with the president to advise him on logistical and cultural issues related to a given visit. Setting the table for diplomacy—literally and figuratively—was a top priority, and I cherished finding that nuanced element that might offer my president the advantage at that table.

Diplomatic protocol has two main goals: to bridge, or establish a connection, and to persuade, to pull your counterpart over to your line of thinking. Every element the protocol team considers is in service of one or both of these engines of diplomacy. Protocol officers look at elements like room size (is the selected room so vast that the leader feels swallowed?) seating (are the chairs not particularly comfortable, encouraging the leaders to end at the designated time?) and logistics of arrivals and departures (will restricting the movements of press and staff communicate control or annoyance?). Sometimes the goal is an inviting ambience; sometimes the aim is efficiency—but respect is always paramount. Protocol teams never use their tools to “spear” their counterpart or deliberately introduce problems; we seek to find moments to enhance our side’s advantage. Above all, we look for ways to elevate the discussion so that all participants can operate at their best.

When Obama and Putin met at the 2012 G-20 Summit in Los Cabos, Mexico, my team and I wielded specific tools of protocol to engage Putin in ways that conveyed respect but also gave us a negotiation edge. The overarching goal was to move the relationship away from distrust and toward understanding and collaboration, a continuation of our effort to reset the U.S.-Russia relationship.

The United States was hosting the meeting, so many elements were under our control. We chose a small room, to push the two leaders into closer proximity and create the sense of a collaborative environment. Toward the same end, we picked a narrow table to allow for a more intimate discussion and introduced a feeling of warmth with lamps and table arrangements of greenery (soothing color, no scent).

All of these micro details can make that subtle difference—and in this case, I believe they did. After the two-hour meeting, the leaders emerged from the room with handshakes and pats on the back. It was clear they were exhausted and that major policy differences remained, but the meeting was deemed a step in the right direction, with Obama and Putin agreeing on a joint statement calling for a cessation of violence in Syria.

Small deviations from protocol also speak volumes. Putin arrived to the meeting late, a favorite tactic of his to throw the other side off-balance. But two can play at the game of the unexpected. We knew Putin hoped Secretary of State Hillary Clinton would not be in attendance (she had commented unfavorably on his election). Obama recognized the rift as an opportunity. During introductions, he stepped aside to reveal Clinton’s presence, and surprise flashed across Putin’s face for a split second. The moment was brief, but it threw Putin off, and telegraphed Obama’s resolve.

Biden will arrive in Geneva after a long but successful week of diplomatic engagements, each guided by their own intricate sets of protocol. The moment Air Force One lands on the tarmac, the geopolitical tensions between the United States and Russia will be sizzling on the front burners. The looming distrust will color the discussion, even as both leaders understand the need to seek out closer alignment on many global crises, from Covid-19 to nuclear proliferation to climate change. For each side’s protocol team, every element, every moment will matter.

Let’s start with location. A well-chosen venue can help turn rivals into partners or persuade nations to get behind a war effort. In 1985, the grand Geneva château Fleur d’Eau was chosen for the first meeting between President Ronald Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev, which helped kick off a thaw in the U.S.-Soviet relationship. The serene, comfortable environment offered the two men an opening to learn about the other’s personal life.

Today, again, Geneva is the host city. Importantly, it’s a neutral location: The Swiss are seasoned hosts, and know how to avoid showing favor to one side or the other. The specific meeting site is the Villa La Grange. Steeped in history, the centuries-old estate is secluded within a lush, dense park (this helps with security concerns) and functions today as a bibliothèque, a peaceful site of learning.

Once the site is chosen, it’s time for weeks of location planning. Multiple conference calls are held in the Situation Room. The U.S. delegation visits the site about a month in advance. They will have held meetings with the Swiss and, perhaps, with the Russians, to iron out the details of the visit. The chief of protocol will have reached out to his or her counterparts from both countries, and to the ambassadors of Switzerland and Russia as a courtesy. (The Biden administration’s current acting chief of protocol, Asel Roberts, is a long-serving career diplomat who speaks Russian fluently, a huge advantage for this particular bilateral!)

By now, the advance team of U.S. protocol officers have scouted the villa for all necessary intel: exits/entries, staff and leader holding rooms, distances from one room to the next, security checkpoints, even the number and location of restrooms. Knowing every aspect of the location prepares the protocol officer for the unknown issues that are bound to arise and to answer any question the leaders may ask.

Next up is room staging. You want your leader’s seat facing the door, or to at least allow eyes on the door, as this limits surprises and facilitates visual communication (a nod, a lift of the eyebrow) with staff who enter.

I’m certain the protocol teams are also considering what is on the table—the centerpieces (perhaps a particular item facilitates or detracts from a talking point, like a piece of art that sends a message about climate change), colors (do they brighten or dampen the mood?), types of flowers (allergy alert! And scented varieties can distract the leaders). What should the lighting be like (bright and cheery or dim and intimate)? Security details often shield the windows, so natural light may be out: The team may need to bring in warm, ambient illumination to reinforce a friendlier quality. The details have been negotiated and renegotiated. Perfect implementation is key.

Other considerations: Gifts. Gifts are a valuable soft-power tool, a means to convey a message that words cannot. The standard at most summits is to exchange the gifts between protocol officers behind the scenes. Yet, as I discovered the hard way, you need a contingency plan when a leader deviates from expectations. The Russians, known for the unexpected, like to color outside the lines in the gift-giving department as well. In Prague in 2010, President Dmitry Medvedev pre-empted protocol by presenting his gift to Obama at the end of the two leaders’ meeting (we call this out-gifting). And at the Helsinki summit between Putin and President Donald Trump, the Russian leader surprised Trump by pulling a soccer ball out of his bag while speaking to the press at a podium. The purpose quickly became clear—to divert attention from questions on Syria. This time, the two presidents have no plans to appear together before cameras, so we may never know whether Putin pulled something out of his bag.

Because we are still living in pandemic times, layered onto the normal summit preparations are the multiple Covid-19 guidelines each delegation will negotiate. Will masks be worn? Will food be served or beverages only? Covid protocols are necessary and should be followed, but they certainly present challenges to protocol officers because they have the potential to disrupt the respectful social codes of conduct that guide leaders. Moreover, diplomacy often depends on the split-second decisions leaders make based on their counterpart’s facial reactions. Masks may make it more difficult for them to gauge in real time where to take the discussion.

Finally, an indispensable tool in the protocol toolbox is diplomatic agility. The best-laid protocol plans can be wiped out in a moment’s notice during this diplomatic dance. Being open to detours (and the Russians will have detours) allows delegates to swerve when they have to. I don’t recall a single international summit—whether a G-8, G-20, NATO or any other convening—that did not have a surprise off-script moment. From having a wreath fall apart minutes before the U.S. president was to lay it at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier to honor a visiting leader (I had to reconstruct it leaf by leaf in 60 seconds), to finding a way for leaders to sneak a cigarette break in a no-smoking building, to regaining my composure after tumbling down the White House’s North Portico steps in front of two world leaders, protocol teaches you to swerve and adjust.

Protocol protects leaders from the potholes and sets expectations: where to walk, what will be in the room, the format for the engagement, the cultural issues to be aware of, what to avoid. Leaders are briefed repeatedly. Preparation quells their anxiety and provides emotional stability. When even one detail is out of sorts, it can throw diplomats off their game (a misspelled name plate, a missing chair at the table) and create confusion, mistrust or embarrassment (when Trump mistakenly saluted a North Korean general, it resulted in a flustered U.S. delegation, a diversion in press attention from the issues, and a visible snicker from an adversarial leader).

The best diplomats use verbal and nonverbal tools to finesse the issues, to deflect ultimatums and to draw reluctant counterparts to the table while retaining a position of strength. It can be like watching an exquisite ballet. For a skilled leader, the tools of protocol can deliver that critical edge, eliminating the stressors that can throw off a high-stakes meeting, making the atmosphere in the room more conducive to productive discussion—and, sometimes, creating a barely perceptible shift in the balance of power.

Biden and Putin both know there is a great deal at stake in each of the individual interactions that make up the U.S.-Russia relationship. No matter the geopolitical crisis, no matter the weight of uncertainty, it is protocol that carefully and intentionally deploys predictability and mutual respect to hold the world’s weightiest relationships in place.
